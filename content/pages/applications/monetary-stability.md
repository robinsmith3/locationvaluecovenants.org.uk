## Basic concept {#basic-concept}

Location Value Covenants could have an important monetary-policy merit in addition to helping individual households. Properly designed, an LVC can replace part of a conventional mortgage that would otherwise be created by a commercial bank.

Commercial banks create new deposits when they make loans. Mortgage expansion can therefore increase broad money, household leverage and the amount of credit competing for a limited supply of property. The Bank of England [explains that the majority of money is created when commercial banks extend loans](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/speech/2024/may/andrew-bailey-lecture-london-school-of-economics-charles-goodhart).

An LVC offers a different route. Government can finance the public contribution through normal gilt issuance placed with pension funds, insurers, households and other investors using existing savings. If every pound of LVC funding replaces a pound of mortgage lending that would otherwise have been created, existing savings take the place of new bank credit.

### The crucial distinction

Gilt issuance is not automatically deflationary. Investors first pay government for the gilts, but government then pays the LVC contribution into the property transaction. The withdrawal and expenditure broadly offset one another.

The monetary advantage arises from the **mortgage credit displaced**, not simply from issuing government debt.

The appropriate policy test is:

**Every £1 of new LVC funding should replace at least £1 of conventional mortgage credit that would otherwise have been created.**

## Worked example {#worked-example}

A household purchases a £300,000 property with a £60,000 deposit.

| Source of purchase funds | Conventional mortgage | LVC structure |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Existing household deposit | £60,000 | £60,000 |
| New commercial-bank mortgage | £240,000 | £120,000 |
| Government LVC contribution | — | £120,000 |
| Total purchasing funds | £300,000 | £300,000 |

Under the conventional transaction, the purchaser pays the full £300,000 unencumbered value using a £60,000 deposit and a £240,000 mortgage.

Under the LVC transaction, the purchaser pays £180,000 using the same deposit and a £120,000 mortgage. The public LVC fund pays the remaining £120,000 to the vendor in exchange for the continuing covenant attached to the property. The vendor therefore still receives the full £300,000 assessed value.

The purchaser has not received an unrequited £120,000 subsidy. The property is acquired subject to a continuing payment linked to the agreed share of its annually assessed location value. That obligation is capitalised into the £120,000 reduction in the price paid by the purchaser and will continue to affect its resale price.

If the public contribution is financed from genuine existing savings, the transaction is completed with £120,000 less newly created commercial-bank mortgage credit while the public fund acquires a continuing location-value asset.

### What happens to the public money

The Treasury does not issue a separate gilt for each home. Parliament would authorise an LVC programme, and its aggregate cash requirement would form part of normal government financing. A public LVC fund would pay the approved contribution through the purchaser’s bank or conveyancer when the covenant was registered.

Government receives a continuing public asset in exchange: the right to collect the property’s LVC payment from successive owners. In the early years, new contributions would exceed covenant receipts. As the covenanted property base grew, its receipts could finance an increasing share of new LVCs, administration and other public purposes.

### How the public contribution is valued

The government does not make an unrecoverable contribution. It acquires a legally enforceable, continuing revenue-producing asset attached to the property.

The capital value of that asset is the discounted value of its expected future net payments:

**LVC value = Σ [expected net payment in year t ÷ (1 + r)^t]**

For a payment expected to continue indefinitely and grow at a constant rate, this can be simplified to:

**LVC value = P ÷ (r − g)**

where:

- **P** is the expected net covenant payment in the first year;
- **r** is the discount rate, including financing cost and risk; and
- **g** is the expected long-term growth rate of covenant payments.

For example, a covenant payment of £500 per month produces £6,000 in its first year. Using an illustrative 5% discount rate and assuming no real payment growth:

**£6,000 ÷ 0.05 = £120,000**

The covenant therefore has an illustrative capital value of £120,000. On a property worth £300,000 without the covenant:

- The public LVC fund pays £120,000 to the vendor.
- The purchaser pays the remaining £180,000.
- The vendor still receives the full £300,000.
- The purchaser and future owners assume the continuing covenant payment.

In practice, the calculation would use expected payments after allowing for administration, arrears, collection costs and other risks. The discount rate would also reflect government financing costs and uncertainty in future location values.

### The continuing payment

The covenant payment withdraws disposable income from the property owner in a similar way to a tax payment. It is most counter-inflationary when receipts are used to reduce borrowing, retire debt or build the LVC fund rather than being immediately added to unrelated expenditure.

If all receipts are immediately spent or used for broad tax reductions, much of that demand restraint is returned to the economy. The fiscal use of covenant revenue therefore matters.

### How covenant revenue is used {#revenue-use}

Continuing LVC payments initially reduce private disposable income. Whether this restrains aggregate demand depends on the government's overall fiscal response. If the revenue finances additional spending or immediate tax reductions, the short-term demand effect may be partly or wholly offset. If it reduces borrowing or is accumulated during inflationary periods, more of the restraint remains.

This produces two distinct policy opportunities:

- **Short-run demand management.** During periods of excessive inflation, covenant revenue could be used to reduce government borrowing or accumulated temporarily in a stabilisation fund. This would preserve more of the immediate restraint on aggregate demand.
- **Long-run structural reform.** Covenant revenue could replace taxes that penalise employment, profits, investment and productive activity. Such tax substitution may not reduce aggregate demand immediately, but it could expand productive capacity, improve economic efficiency and reduce inflationary pressure over time.

The appropriate use of covenant revenue could therefore vary with economic conditions. During an inflationary period, the priority might be demand restraint. As inflation subsides, more of the revenue could be used to replace economically damaging taxes.

## Policy effect {#policy-effect}

### Why this strengthens the case for adoption

- **Less bank-created mortgage credit:** households can purchase with smaller commercial loans.
- **Lower household leverage:** less principal is exposed to compound interest, refinancing and negative equity.
- **Reduced interest-rate transmission:** household budgets become less sensitive to changes in mortgage rates.
- **A broader public balance sheet:** government acquires a continuing revenue asset rather than making an unrequited grant.
- **Long-term savings put to productive use:** gilts can connect pension and insurance savings with housing-finance reform.
- **A gradual transition:** conventional mortgages remain available while voluntary adoption expands the covenant system.
- **Potential monetary stabilisation:** slower mortgage-credit growth can moderate one source of property-driven monetary expansion.

This could appeal simultaneously to households seeking lower-risk finance, governments seeking continuing revenue, and policymakers concerned with excessive leverage and financial instability.

### Why would each participant choose this?

* **Homeowner — why would I do this?** I choose a lower capital price and smaller mortgage in exchange for a continuing location-value payment and reduced exposure to future gains in the covenanted share of the location. I retain the value of the building, my improvements and any uncovenanted location share.
* **Government:** It acquires a continuing public revenue asset while replacing part of the bank-created mortgage credit that would otherwise finance the purchase. This can reduce household leverage and improve monetary and financial resilience.
* **Bank:** It provides a smaller, lower loan-to-value mortgage with less default and negative-equity risk. It retains a lending and customer-service role, although it earns interest on a smaller principal.

The homeowner’s benefit is not free public money. The public contribution purchases the continuing covenant interest and reduces the capital price accordingly. The homeowner chooses greater affordability and lower financial risk today in exchange for a continuing payment and a smaller claim on future location value.

### Conditions required for the benefit

The result depends on the LVC being treated as the purchase of a continuing public covenant asset, not as a grant or buyer subsidy. A monetary-stability programme should require:

1. The purchaser’s reduced capital price to reflect the value of the continuing covenant obligation attached to the property.
2. The public contribution to purchase a corresponding covenant interest rather than add to the purchaser’s net bidding capacity.
3. Independent valuation separating the value of buildings and owner-funded improvements from the value attributable to the land and location.
4. The continuing payment to remain linked to the agreed share of annually assessed location value.
5. Each LVC contribution to replace commercial mortgage lending that would otherwise be required to finance the same unencumbered property value.
6. Gilt funding to be directed primarily to genuine non-bank savings rather than associated monetary financing.
7. Gradual issuance informed by inflation, mortgage-credit growth, housing supply and financial stability.
8. Covenant receipts to be retained or used to reduce public borrowing when aggregate demand is excessive.
9. Coordination between HM Treasury, the Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority, the public valuation authorities and the Debt Management Office.

### Risks that must be controlled

An LVC is not equivalent to a grant, subsidised loan or conventional buyer-assistance scheme. The purchaser accepts a continuing payment linked to the annually assessed location value, and that liability should be reflected in a correspondingly lower capital price. A larger LVC therefore produces both a larger public contribution and a larger continuing obligation; it does not provide equivalent additional net purchasing power with which to bid up the property.

The principal property-market risk is incorrect or inconsistent valuation. The system must ensure that the purchaser is not charged both the full unencumbered capital value and the continuing LVC payment. Independent valuations should distinguish clearly between:

- The value of buildings and improvements;
- The unencumbered location value;
- The proportion of location value covered by the LVC;
- The resulting reduction in the purchaser’s capital price; and
- The continuing payment associated with the covenanted share.

Owner-funded buildings and improvements must remain outside the annual LVC assessment. The continuing payment should rise or fall only with the independently assessed value attributable to the location.

Lower mortgage payments may leave households with more disposable income, which could increase consumption elsewhere in the economy. Additional gilt issuance may also affect government borrowing costs and wider interest rates. These macroeconomic effects should be assessed against the larger mortgage and greater credit creation that the LVC replaces.

### A defensible monetary-policy claim

Location Value Covenants should not be promoted on the assertion that selling gilts itself removes money permanently from circulation. Government expenditure returns the proceeds to the private sector.

The stronger proposition is:

**Location Value Covenants can substitute long-term public funding drawn from existing savings for newly created commercial-bank mortgage credit. Properly limited and valued, this can restrain credit and money growth, reduce household leverage and moderate property-driven inflation while creating a continuing public revenue asset.**

This makes monetary stability a significant additional merit of LVC adoption—not a guaranteed outcome, but a design opportunity capable of being measured, piloted and independently tested.

### Evidence required from a pilot

A pilot should report:

- The amount of LVC funding issued.
- The mortgage lending directly displaced.
- The independently assessed value of buildings and improvements.
- The independently assessed unencumbered location value.
- The purchaser’s reduced capital price and the public LVC contribution.
- Whether resale prices continue to reflect the capitalised covenant obligation.
- Annual changes in assessed location value and continuing payments.
- Covenant receipts, administration costs, appeals and arrears.
- Effects on household monthly outgoings and disposable income.
- The source of gilt purchasers and the programme’s financing cost.
- The net effect on public borrowing, credit growth and the wider economy.

The Bank of England notes that higher interest rates reduce spending and inflation, while bond purchases designed to lower long-term rates support spending. That illustrates why the LVC’s monetary effect must be judged from the whole transaction rather than from gilt issuance alone.
